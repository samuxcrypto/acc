The Complete Accounting Cycle

A connected November practice question with a fully worked solution: start with the accounting equation, record journal entries, post to T-accounts, prepare trial balances, adjust the accounts, and finish all four financial statements.

Part 1 — The practice question

Business: Sami Services Ltd.   Accounting period: 1–30 November 2026

Sami Services Ltd. is a newly started service business. During November, the following transactions took place. Assume the business started on 1 November, so all opening balances are zero.

  2. One month of the 12-month insurance policy has expired.
  3. Equipment depreciation for November is $200.
  4. Employees have earned a further $500 in salaries, but this amount will be paid in December.

Your tasks

  1. Show the effect of each transaction on the accounting equation.
  2. Prepare the general journal.
  3. Post the journal entries to ledger T-accounts.
  4. Prepare the unadjusted trial balance.
  5. Prepare the adjusting journal entries.
  6. Prepare the adjusted trial balance.
  7. Prepare the statement of cash flows.
  8. Prepare the income statement.
  9. Prepare the statement of retained earnings.
  10. Prepare the balance sheet.
Important assumptions: This is a simplified introductory exercise. There are no taxes, interest charges, accounts payable, or other transactions. The bank loan is treated as a liability. Dividends are distributions to shareholders, not an expense.

Part 2 — Understand the accounting equation

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity

Assets are what the business owns or controls (cash, receivables, supplies, equipment). Liabilities are amounts it owes (bank loan, unearned revenue, salaries payable). Equity is the owners’ residual interest (share capital plus retained earnings).

Every transaction must keep both sides equal. Here is the effect of each transaction. “+” means an increase and “−” means a decrease.

Check: The equation balances after every transaction. For example, on Nov 2, assets increase by $10,000 and liabilities also increase by $10,000. Equity does not change.

Why these debit and credit choices?

Remember: A T-account has debits on the left and credits on the right. The balance is the difference between the two sides. An asset or expense usually has a debit balance; a liability, equity, or revenue usually has a credit balance.

How to understand the cash flow adjustments

  • Add depreciation: it reduced accounting profit but did not use cash in November.
  • Subtract increases in receivables, supplies and prepaid insurance: cash has not been received for receivables, and cash was spent to acquire supplies and prepaid insurance.
  • Add increases in unearned revenue and salaries payable: cash was received before earning all the revenue, and some salary expense has not yet been paid.
  • Investing: buying long-term equipment is an investing cash outflow.
  • Financing: share issue and borrowing bring in cash; dividends paid take cash out.
Cross-check: $3,900 − $12,000 + $39,000 = $30,900. This equals the Cash balance in the adjusted trial balance and ledger.

Part 12 — The full process, in exam order

  1. Read the transaction: identify which accounts are affected.
  2. Accounting equation: decide whether assets, liabilities or equity increase/decrease.
  3. General journal: record the debit and credit.
  4. Ledger/T-accounts: post each debit and credit into its account.
  5. Unadjusted trial balance: list balances and check debit total = credit total.
  6. Adjusting entries: record used-up assets, accrued expenses and revenue earned from advances.
  7. Adjusted trial balance: update balances and check equality again.
  8. Cash flow statement: classify cash movements as operating, investing or financing.
  9. Income statement: calculate net income.
  10. Statement of retained earnings: calculate closing retained earnings.
  11. Balance sheet: show assets, liabilities and equity; confirm the equation balances.
Common exam traps
  • Receiving cash in advance is initially a liability, not revenue.
  • Providing a service on credit creates accounts receivable and revenue.
  • Collecting a receivable is not new revenue; it is just cash replacing receivables.
  • Buying equipment is not an immediate expense; it is an asset. Depreciation is recorded over time.
  • Supplies become an expense when used, not simply when purchased.
  • Dividends are not an operating expense and do not appear on the income statement.
  • A trial balance can balance even when an entire transaction has been omitted or recorded in the wrong account.
